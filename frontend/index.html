How this works

The simulator moves every birth year forward one year at a time, for women and men separately, starting from the United Nations population projection. People in work below the retirement age pay contributions. People at or above it receive a pension.

Each year the contributions are compared with the pensions. A gap is covered in the way you choose: by borrowing, by a higher contribution rate, or by a lower benefit level. Finances do not feed back into births, deaths, migration, or employment.

Behind the charts is a standard model of demography, the McKendrick–von Foerster equation, joined to the accounts of the pension system. The simulator solves the equations below in steps of one year, from 2025 to 2100, each time you move a control.

Population

∂n∂t+∂n∂a=−μ(a,t)n(a,t)+m(a,t)
n(0,t)=∫f(a,t)nwomen(a,t)da

n(a, t) is the number of people of age a at time t. People grow older, die at the rate μ, and migrate at the net rate m. Births follow the fertility rate f of women.

Contributions and pensions

C(t)=τ(t)w(t)∫0R(t)e(a)n(a,t)da
B(t)=∫R(t)∞b(a,t)n(a,t)da

People below the retirement age R(t) work at the employment rate e and pay the contribution rate τ on the average wage w. Each person above it receives a pension b.

Pension debt

D(t+1)=(1+r)D(t)−[C(t)−B(t)]
D(2025)=0

The gap of each year is added to the debt D, which carries the interest rate r.

Generations

∑alc(a)pension(a)−contribution(a)(1+i)a=0

For people born in year c, the rate of return i is the rate at which their pensions and contributions cancel over a lifetime. lc(a) is their chance of being alive at age a.
